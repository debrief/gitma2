Gate H trained nothing. It asked whether realistic bearing errors leave room worth learning
for: once the errors are correlated, biased, mis-scaled and include wild values, how much better
is an estimator that is told the true error model than one with textbook robust weighting? On a
fresh test seed the answer is: not measurably. The review panel upheld the verdict and narrowed
what it may be taken to mean. A comparison it asked for then showed where the mismatch does
hurt: in the segmentation, by a factor of two.

## The question

Every learned component in this project so far has turned out, under its control, to be a simple
classical rule ([research note 10](https://github.com/debrief/gitma2/blob/main/docs/research/10-reflection-headroom.md)).
The suspected reason was that our simulated bearing errors are exactly the errors the estimator
assumes: white, Gaussian, with a known σ. Where an estimator's model is the truth, there is
nothing left to learn. So before any more learning work, the owner asked for a measurement: make
the errors unlike the model, and see how much an estimator that *knows* the true error model
gains over one that does not.

## What was compared

Bearing error became the sum of four parts, each of which can be switched on alone:

| Kind | Central severity |
| --- | --- |
| Correlated in time | half the variance, 60 s correlation time |
| Slow bias | 0.5°, wandering over 30 min |
| Untold σ | true σ within a factor 1.5 of the stated 1° |
| Outliers | 2% of bearings, 10 σ |

The severities are a declared grid the owner accepted, not measured sensor figures. The gate is
decided on all four together.

Three estimators share one solve (a least-squares fit of constant-velocity legs) and are given
the true manoeuvre times:

- the **plain solve**, which assumes white noise;
- a **classical robust control**: Huber weights against wild bearings, σ estimated from the
  residuals, and a correlation model fitted to the residuals. Nothing is learned;
- an **error-model-informed fit**: the same solve told the true error covariance. The records
  call this the "attainable ceiling"; the panel found that name overstates it (below).

The statistic is R: the control's median range error over the informed fit's, minus 1. The owner
fixed the threshold before any data existed: headroom is material if R's interval lies wholly
above 10%.

## The result

Test seed 322, 5,203 engagements, 20,473 scored rows, all four kinds at central severity:

**R = −0.4%, 97.5% interval −2.4% to +1.4%. Not material.**

| | Plain solve | Robust control | Informed fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median relative range error | 0.134 | 0.118 | 0.119 |
| Median range error | 2,110 m | 1,880 m | 1,885 m |
| Median course error | 13.3° | 11.2° | 11.3° |
| Median speed error | 1.82 kt | 1.50 kt | 1.47 kt |
| Range within 10% by zig + 10 min | 22% | 26% | 27% |

The plain solve is 12.9% worse than the informed fit. The robust control closes all of that gap.
Both soundness checks passed on the validation seed before the test seed was generated. The
forecast, written from the tuning seed, gave this verdict a probability of 0.85.

By kind, on 1,000 engagements each: outliers alone hurt the plain solve by 43% and Huber weights
remove nearly all of it (R +4.2%); correlated error raises every estimator's error and leaves the
most (R +3.1%); a slow bias costs little (R +1.2%).

## What the panel found

Five reviewers (statistics, estimation, domain, process, red team) each approved the verdict
with findings; none raised a blocker. Three recomputed R from the per-row arrays and matched it.
The process reviewer regenerated the test data from a clean clone and matched every digest.
Their findings change how the result should be read.

**The pooled zero is two effects cancelling.** By true range:

| True range | R |
| --- | --- |
| under 10.7 km | +4.9% [+1.8, +8.2] |
| 10.7 to 17.2 km | +3.0% [−0.2, +7.1] |
| 17.2 to 25.3 km | −2.6% [−5.7, +0.7] |
| over 25.3 km | −5.2% [−8.3, −2.1] |

At short range knowing the error model is worth about 5%. At long range the informed fit is
*worse* than the robust control.

**The reference is not a ceiling.** Both the control and the informed fit sit about 25% above the
Cramér–Rao bound for known manoeuvre times. That gap is larger still with no mismatch at all, so
it is not error-model headroom, but it is room that a better motion model or an end-to-end
estimator could claim. An earlier version of the record explained it by the solve fitting each
manoeuvre with a chord. The estimation reviewer tested that and it accounts for only a part; the
rest is unexplained.

**The severity grid came close to fixing the outcome.** For the Gaussian part of the primary
condition, a linearised calculation puts the plain solve only about 3% behind exact generalised
least squares. Within this error family only outliers could reach 10%, and Huber weights remove
those. Stretching the grid (a 180 s correlation time, or 90% of the variance correlated) gives
8 to 12%.

**The simulated mismatch is the robust control's own model family.** The errors are stationary,
independent of the geometry, with zero-mean independent outliers. Real bearing error is tied to
ownship heading, relative bearing and the zig. The domain reviewer's candidates for hidden
headroom: a bias that steps or lags at the ownship zig (range comes from the bearing-rate change
across the zig), bursty or one-sided outliers, and σ that varies within an engagement.

**The true manoeuvre times hide the likeliest operational cost.** Correlated error and a
wandering bias can look like target manoeuvres. Whether our segmenter degrades under mismatch
was a registered comparison that had not been run when the verdict was computed.

## On our own segmenter's output

The comparison the panel asked for, on the validation seed (1,000 engagements), reported and
not gated:

| Median relative range error | No mismatch | Primary condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Robust control, true manoeuvre times | 0.060 | 0.114 |
| C″: our segmenter, plain solve | 0.102 | 0.280 |
| C″ + robust control | 0.103 | 0.230 |

With the true manoeuvre times, mismatch doubles the error. On our segmenter's own output it
nearly triples it: C recovers 0.92 of the gap between one leg and the oracle on clean data and
0.66 under mismatch. The robust control helps (−17.6% [−22.2, −13.7]), but it only treats the
final solve, and a factor of two remains (3,460 m against 1,710 m in median range error).

So the gate's verdict and this table are both true and answer different questions. Knowing the
error model is worth nothing measurable once the segmentation is right. Getting the segmentation
right under mismatch is worth a factor of two. It is not yet evidence for learning: C was tuned
on clean data and run unchanged, and the obvious first fix is classical.

## What it means

The statement the panel accepted:

> On the true segmentation, under the declared stationary Gaussian-plus-outlier bearing-error
> family at the stated severities, knowing the true error model is worth no more than about 1.5%
> (median) to 3% (P90) in range error to the piecewise-constant-velocity least-squares solve,
> over a textbook robust fit. Learning the bearing-error model for this solve is not pursued.

Not shown: that this solve is near the best possible; that segmentation survives mismatch;
anything about other error families or real data.

The SRD row said that with this verdict "the learning track on this estimator stops". The
evidence supports the narrower sentence above. Whether the learning track as a whole stops is
the owner's decision. One useful by-product stands regardless: the robust control is a cheap,
classical 12% improvement over the plain solve under these errors, and costs under 2% where
nothing is wrong.

## What was not achieved

- The comparison on our own segmenter's output was promised and run late, after the verdict, on
  the validation seed and for the combined condition only. Which kind of mismatch breaks the
  segmenter, and whether it nominates falsely or misses, is not measured.
- The cause of the solve's 25% distance from the bound.
- Target-motion mismatch, other error families, a classical bias state, real data.
- The IMM and EKF references are not competitive on these rows (median range error near 0.6)
  and leave a quarter unanswered; they were re-tuned in one pass only.

## Process notes

- Criteria, plan and decision record were committed together before either the validation or the
  test seed was generated. Validation and test ran from the pull request's branch on the owner's
  instruction while the SRD amendment was still open; that is recorded in SRD §9.
- Two of the gate's checks were shaped after tuning results existed and are disclosed: the form
  of the clean-data check (the first draft could not be decided at the sample size; the owner
  chose its replacement) and the efficiency band.
- The author's first explanation of the efficiency gap was wrong and is withdrawn in the record.
- The reviewers are agents of the same model family as the author.

## Next

1. **Recommended first, because it is cheap and trains nothing: a robust classical segmenter.**
   Give C the robust control's σ and correlation estimates and Huber costs in its own tests,
   measure what is left of the factor of two, and find which kind of mismatch causes it. That
   is the control any learned segmenter would have to beat. If the owner does not object, it is
   adopted as the next spec.
2. **The scaled end-to-end regressor** (research note 04, option B), already decided by the
   owner: was gitma's 3 × bound a matter of compute? It needs a Modal account and two repository
   secrets from the owner. The 25% between this solve and the bound, and the factor of two on
   the segmenter's output, give it something to win.
3. A zig-coupled bias and bursty outliers (the domain reviewer's proposal), on the same harness.
4. Find out why the solve is 25% above the bound.
