Gate 2: E beats the classical segmenter, mostly by rules a designer could write

November 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Gate 2 asked whether learning, trained end to end through the physics layer, does better than the classical segmenter C on the owner's manoeuvring scenarios. On the fresh test seed it passes all four checks. The learned model E recovers more of the range-error gap between a one-leg fit and the oracle than C does: +0.025, with a 97.5% interval of [+0.018, +0.031].

The agent review panel then asked what E had actually learned. Most of it turned out to be two simple rules. The first is to raise the bar for a target turn at the ownship's own manoeuvre. The second is to trust recent bearings more. Those two rules, with nothing learned, reproduce about 72% of E's margin over C. Learning adds a small but real increment beyond them: +0.007 [+0.001, +0.012].

What was tested​

C is Gate S's classical segmenter, frozen by digest (see steady-leg segmentation). It nominates target transitions, confirms them by the coupled physics-layer fit, and hands each prefix to the physics layer. E adds learning at C's two decision points (see the Gate 2 model):

  • E-s: a causal evidence network that shifts C's confirmation threshold, by at most τ/2 either way. It is trained on decision tables, which hold each near-threshold decision's downstream error with the transition and without it.
  • E-w: a causal transformer that adds per-bearing log-weights and a start correction to C's hand-over, trained through the physics layer with 12 knots.

Untrained, E is C, to the last bit. Three seeds of each part were trained on stream seed 318, with a shuffled-label control. On validation every seed passed every check, and the median seed (e-s3) was frozen before the test seed was generated.

The decision record fixed everything before training: the four criteria (G2-d added by the owner on 6 November), the limits, the carried-seed, shuffled and validation rules, and a forecast. The forecast was E − C +0.005 [−0.005, +0.015], with a pass judged about a one-in-four chance.

Range and course are medians over all rows (a row with no estimate counts as infinitely wrong). Efficiency is the median error over the known-manoeuvres Cramér–Rao bound.

Where the gain sits:

  • By row type. It is largest on the 5.8% of rows that end inside an open transition, which are C's weakest: +0.139 [+0.077, +0.202]. On the other 94% it is +0.023 [+0.016, +0.028].
  • By range. It grows with range, from nothing below 5 km to +0.05 beyond 30 km.
  • Gross errors. The share of rows with range error over 50% falls from 11.5% to 10.0%.

The gain is stable. It is +0.020 to +0.024 at each of the four scored times, and removing any one scenario moves it by at most 0.0003.

What E learned​

Round 1 of the panel looked inside the two learned parts:

  • E-s puts almost all its evidence within a few rows of the ownship zig. It makes no distinction between rows inside true target manoeuvres and rows outside them. In effect it learned not to accept a target turn exactly when the ownship turns. A target knot at the only ownship manoeuvre lets the target's velocity absorb the bearing-rate change, and range becomes unobservable.
  • E-w learned mostly a recency profile. It raises the log-weights of the newest bearings by about 0.7 and lowers those of bearings half an hour old by about as much.

So the panel asked for classical versions of both, registered before any of their results existed:

  • C′: C with a veto. Candidates at the ownship zig ± 6 rows get E-s's largest threshold shift. The window was chosen on E-s's own tuning tables.
  • fade: C with a fixed recency profile, clip(0.73 − 0.0079 × age, ±2), read off E-w's mean weights.
  • C″: both rules together, with nothing learned.

Reading these as registered:

  • The two rules reproduce about 72% of E's margin over C. Both rules took their settings from the learned models. So 72% is an upper bound on what a designer would get without E, and E − C″ is a conservative test of learning.
  • Learning adds +0.007 beyond the rules. It is small and rests on one test look. The validation split, five times smaller, is inconclusive (+0.008 [−0.004, +0.017]). The result survives a correction for the six comparisons made against controls.
  • E-s adds nothing shown beyond the zig veto.
  • On test, the increment is consistent with E-w doing more than a fixed fade. On validation that comparison points the other way (−0.003), and its test value was seen before C″ was registered. It is descriptive only.

What the panel found​

Five reviewers covered TMA geometry, estimation and numerics, evaluation statistics, reproducibility and a red team. A sixth, causality audit ran before any test result existed. The panel took two rounds.

  • Causality. Replacing every bearing after the scored row with noise leaves E's estimate at that row bit-identical. There is no leak, and a test now enforces this.
  • The numbers. Statistics recomputed every gated number from the per-row errors, bit for bit, and with an independent bootstrap. Reproducibility regenerated seed 319 byte for byte and checked every digest and the order of events: decision, carried model, test seed, plans, results.
  • Two majors in round 1 (E-s is mostly a zig veto; E-w mostly a fading memory). Both were answered by the classical controls above and closed in round 2.
  • Wording. Round 2's findings were about wording: no claim that E-s is a zig veto, the attribution to E-w resting on test alone, and where the rules' settings came from.

All findings and responses are in review.md.

Caveats​

  • The shuffled control could not void G2-d as run. Both shuffled parts stopped at epoch 0, so the shuffled E is C. The real evidence that labels matter is in the training logs: both shuffled runs got worse when trained (E-s 52.3 → 54.0, E-w 38.1 → 44.9), while every real seed improved.
  • No time-late variant was trained. Constitution IV requires one. It is a follow-on.
  • The score depends slightly on the CPU. The scorer re-solves C and the references. On a different CPU family C's errors move by up to 6e-4 relative and G2-d by 1.4e-5. E's errors, read from its prediction file, do not move.
  • Every transition is handed over as a course change. The speed prior applies even across speed changes. This affects C and E alike, so it does not touch G2-d, but it costs both of them against the oracle.
  • C is still 2.7 times the bound, and E 2.5. The oracle, at 1.6, shows what knowing the change times is worth.

What follows​

The SRD leaves the follow-on to the owner, with a recommended default. Recommended:

  • Carry E (e-s3) as the estimator of record, and keep C″ (zig veto + fade) as a standing classical control in every later gate.
  • Next is SRD §7 step 5's first item, uncertainty calibration (spec 008). E-w and the fade rescale the bearing weights, so their covariances are not on C's scale and must be calibrated before any covariance-based gating.
  • Spec 008 also takes the panel's follow-ons:
    • a time-late variant;
    • scoring the last-epoch shuffled weights;
    • coverage assertions in the scorer;
    • storing C's scored errors instead of re-solving them.

This is adopted if the owner has not objected by the time spec 008 is written.
